SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 01

01. What Is Fire?

Fire is a rapid, self-sustaining exothermic oxidation process in which a combustible material reacts with an oxidizer and releases energy as heat, light, and chemical products. In an ordinary building fire, the oxidizer is atmospheric oxygen, but the broader chemistry of combustion is not restricted to oxygen. The word fire therefore describes a physical-chemical event, not merely the visible flame.

A useful engineering description is a coupled system: chemical reaction generates heat; heat is transferred by conduction, convection, and radiation; the transferred heat decomposes and vaporizes additional fuel; buoyancy moves hot gases; and the resulting flow changes the supply of oxidizer. This feedback is why a fire can grow rather than simply remain at the size of its ignition flame.

For fire forensics, this distinction is fundamental. A damaged object is the final state of a dynamic thermal history. Charring, melting, soot deposition, deformation, cracking, and loss of material cannot be interpreted correctly without asking what heat-transfer and combustion processes could have produced them.

02. Combustion and Fire

Combustion is the chemical process; fire is the larger physical event in which combustion becomes sufficiently energetic and self-sustaining to produce a characteristic thermal environment. Oxidation can occur very slowly without visible flame. Conversely, a visible flame is only one region of the combustion system and may not coincide with the surface where the original fuel is located.

Most common fires involve gas-phase combustion. A piece of wood does not normally burn because the solid carbon directly reacts with atmospheric oxygen throughout its volume. Instead, heat first dries and thermally decomposes the wood. Volatile products leave the surface, mix with air, and burn in a flame above the solid. The flame then radiates and convects heat back to the surface, sustaining further pyrolysis.

This explains why fire investigation requires knowledge of both chemistry and heat transfer. The investigator is not merely looking for a 'burn mark'; the task is to determine whether the observed material response is consistent with a physically plausible sequence of heating, pyrolysis, ignition, spread, ventilation, and decay.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 03

03. The Fire Triangle

The fire triangle represents three requirements for sustained combustion: fuel, an oxidizer, and sufficient heat. Fuel supplies chemical energy, the oxidizer permits oxidation, and heat raises the reactants to conditions at which the reaction can continue. If one requirement is removed or reduced below the necessary level, sustained flaming can cease.

The model is deliberately simple. It is useful for teaching and for explaining basic extinguishment mechanisms, but it does not quantify heat release rate, fluid flow, reaction kinetics, fuel geometry, or ventilation. A fire can possess all three elements and still fail to sustain a flame if their concentrations, temperatures, or transport rates are unsuitable.

In forensic reasoning, the triangle is best used as a screening framework. For a proposed ignition sequence, identify the fuel, identify the oxidizer, identify the energy source, and then ask whether the three could interact at the required time and location. The mere presence of an ignition source is not proof that ignition occurred.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 04

04. The Fire Tetrahedron

The fire tetrahedron adds a fourth concept to the triangle: the chemical chain reaction. In a flame, highly reactive intermediate species participate in sequences of reactions that regenerate reactive species and rapidly convert fuel and oxidizer into products. Radicals such as H, O, and OH are important examples in hydrocarbon flames.

The tetrahedron is especially useful for explaining suppression. Water primarily removes energy through heating and vaporization. Foam can separate fuel from the flame and reduce vapour release. Inert gases reduce the effective oxidizer concentration. Some extinguishing agents interfere with flame chemistry. These mechanisms are different, but all ultimately drive the combustion system below the conditions required for self-sustained reaction.

The tetrahedron is still a conceptual model. Real flames involve transport phenomena, turbulence, radiation, hundreds or thousands of elementary reactions in detailed kinetic descriptions, and changing boundary conditions. It should therefore not be treated as a complete mathematical model of fire.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 05

05. Oxidation, Reduction and Combustion Chemistry

Oxidation and reduction are complementary chemical concepts. Oxidation can involve loss of electrons or an increase in oxidation state, while reduction involves the corresponding gain of electrons or decrease in oxidation state. Combustion commonly involves oxidation of a fuel while an oxidizing species is reduced.

For a simplified hydrocarbon fuel CxHy burning completely in oxygen, the overall reaction can be written as CxHy + (x + y/4)O2 → xCO2 + (y/2)H2O. This equation is useful because it establishes stoichiometric relationships, but it does not describe the actual mechanism of a flame. Real combustion proceeds through intermediate species and many elementary reactions.

Incomplete combustion occurs when mixing, temperature, residence time, or oxidizer availability prevent complete conversion. Carbon monoxide, soot, partially oxidized hydrocarbons, aldehydes, and other products may result. Therefore, combustion-product chemistry contains information about fire conditions, but a detected compound normally cannot by itself establish the fire's origin or cause.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 06

06. Reaction Rate and Arrhenius Behaviour

Combustion rate is strongly temperature dependent. A simplified Arrhenius relationship is k = A exp(-Ea/RT), where k is a reaction-rate constant, A is a pre-exponential factor, Ea is activation energy, R is the gas constant, and T is absolute temperature. The equation shows why a relatively small temperature increase can cause a large change in reaction rate when activation energy is significant.

This temperature sensitivity creates an important fire feedback. A fuel surface receives heat, its decomposition rate increases, more combustible vapour is generated, and combustion releases additional heat. If the heat feedback exceeds losses, the process accelerates. If losses dominate, the process may extinguish or remain as slow smouldering.

The Arrhenius equation is a kinetic model, not a direct equation for the burning rate of an entire room. Fire growth also depends on transport, geometry, ventilation, material properties, and available fuel. It is therefore dangerous to use a single reaction-temperature value to explain a complex fire.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 07

07. Fuels: Solids, Liquids and Gases

Fire fuels behave differently according to their physical state. A gaseous fuel can already be in a form suitable for mixing with air. A liquid normally burns through vapour generated above its surface. A solid commonly must be heated and thermally decomposed before producing enough combustible gas to sustain flaming.

For liquids, vapour pressure and temperature determine how readily an ignitable vapour concentration can develop. For solids, density, thickness, moisture, surface area and thermal properties influence heating and pyrolysis. For gases, leakage rate, concentration, confinement and ventilation determine whether a flammable mixture develops.

The investigator should therefore describe the fuel as a physical system rather than simply naming a material. 'Plastic,' 'wood,' or 'fuel' is not enough. The relevant questions include what form it was in, how it was arranged, what surfaces were exposed, whether it was wet or dry, what could heat it, and how combustible vapours could reach an oxidizer.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 08

08. Flash Point, Fire Point and Autoignition

Flash point is the lowest temperature at which a liquid produces sufficient vapour near its surface to form an ignitable mixture under specified test conditions. At the flash point, ignition may produce a transient flash rather than sustained burning. Fire point is generally higher and relates to conditions supporting continued burning under the relevant test method.

Autoignition temperature refers to conditions under which a substance or mixture can ignite without an external pilot. It is not a universal material constant in the simplistic sense often implied in tables. Test apparatus, pressure, concentration, surface effects, heating method, and residence time influence reported values.

These distinctions are important in forensic work. A tabulated flash point does not tell an investigator the temperature of a real spill at the time of ignition, and a tabulated autoignition temperature cannot be inserted into a reconstruction without considering the actual heat-transfer mechanism. Laboratory properties are inputs to reasoning, not conclusions.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 09

09. Flammability Limits and Mixture Composition

A combustible gas or vapour generally requires an appropriate concentration in the oxidizing atmosphere for flame propagation. The lower and upper flammability limits define approximate concentration boundaries under specified test conditions. Below the lower limit there is insufficient fuel; above the upper limit there may be insufficient oxidizer for propagation.

Real environments are not perfectly mixed. Concentration can vary from one location to another, and turbulent flow can create transient pockets with different compositions. Temperature and pressure can also influence flammability behaviour. This is why a gas release may produce ignition in one zone while remaining non-flammable elsewhere.

For forensic reconstruction, the important sequence is release, transport, mixing, ignition, and subsequent flame propagation. Finding a combustible material is only one part of that chain. A credible hypothesis must explain how an ignitable mixture could have existed at the proposed ignition location and time.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 10

10. Heat and Temperature

Temperature describes the thermal state of a material or fluid, while heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. They are related but not interchangeable. A large mass at a moderate temperature can contain much more thermal energy than a tiny object at a very high temperature.

For a simple sensible-heating process, Q = mcΔT, where Q is energy transferred, m is mass, c is specific heat capacity, and ΔT is temperature change. This relationship becomes more complicated when phase change, moisture evaporation, or chemical decomposition occurs.

Fire investigations often encounter the misconception that the highest temperature necessarily identifies the origin. It does not. Temperature depends on exposure duration, ventilation, heat losses, emissivity, material properties, and geometry. A remote surface can experience severe heating after the fire has spread away from its origin, while the original fuel may later be consumed or cooled.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 11

11. Specific Heat, Thermal Conductivity and Diffusivity

Specific heat capacity describes how much energy is required to raise the temperature of a unit mass by one degree. Thermal conductivity describes how readily heat is conducted through a material. Thermal diffusivity, α = k/(ρc), combines conductivity k, density ρ, and specific heat c and indicates how rapidly temperature disturbances propagate through the material.

These properties explain why materials exposed to the same flame can develop very different temperature profiles. A good conductor can move energy away from an exposed region rapidly, while a low-conductivity material may develop a steep temperature gradient. Density and heat capacity determine how much energy is required to change the temperature of the material.

Moisture is particularly important. Before wet material can rise substantially in temperature, energy may be consumed in heating and vaporizing water. Consequently, two apparently identical materials with different moisture content can have substantially different ignition and damage behaviour.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 12

12. Conduction

Conduction is heat transfer through matter driven by a temperature gradient. For one-dimensional steady conduction through a homogeneous material, Fourier's law can be expressed as q'' = -k(dT/dx), where q'' is conductive heat flux, k is thermal conductivity, and dT/dx is the temperature gradient.

In a fire, conduction can transmit heat through doors, walls, pipes, metal components, structural members, and furniture. A surface directly exposed to flames can become hot while the opposite side remains substantially cooler. With time, the thermal disturbance penetrates deeper.

The forensic significance is that damage on one side of an assembly does not necessarily indicate direct flame contact on the other side. Heat may have travelled through the material. Investigators should consider material thickness, conductivity, contact conditions, exposure duration, and possible heat sinks before interpreting thermal damage as evidence of flame location.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 13

13. Convection

Convection transfers energy through moving fluids. In fires, the fluid is commonly a mixture of hot combustion products and entrained air. Hot gases become less dense and rise, creating buoyant flow. The resulting plume transports heat and combustion products upward and outward.

Natural convection is driven primarily by buoyancy. Forced convection results when airflow is imposed by wind, fans, ventilation equipment, pressure differences, or other mechanisms. Real fires can involve both simultaneously.

Convection can heat ceilings, walls, contents, and structural members without direct flame contact. The magnitude of convective heat transfer depends on gas temperature, flow velocity, surface geometry, and a convective heat-transfer coefficient. Because the coefficient varies with the flow regime, a simple temperature observation is not sufficient to calculate damage without additional information.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 14

14. Thermal Radiation

Thermal radiation is electromagnetic energy emitted by matter because of its temperature. It does not require direct physical contact or a moving fluid between source and target. This makes radiation an important mechanism for heating and ignition across gaps.

For an idealized radiating surface, emitted power is related to absolute temperature by the Stefan-Boltzmann law. Real surfaces have emissivity values below one, and practical radiative exchange also depends on geometry and view factor. A target receives only a fraction of the energy emitted by a flame or hot surface.

Radiation can ignite nearby materials without direct flame contact. It can also produce significant preheating that shortens ignition time once a flame reaches the target. Forensic interpretation should therefore consider line-of-sight exposure, distance, flame size, surface orientation, duration, and shielding.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 15

15. Combined Heat Transfer

Conduction, convection and radiation normally occur together. Consider a timber surface beneath a compartment fire: it can receive radiation from flames and hot surfaces, convection from the hot gas layer, and conduction through its own thickness. The surface temperature is determined by the net balance of these mechanisms plus losses.

A useful conceptual surface balance is q''net = q''radiation + q''convection + q''conduction, with signs determined by whether each term adds or removes energy from the surface. Real calculations require appropriate boundary conditions and material properties.

This combined view is critical to fire forensics because visible flame is not the only mechanism capable of producing damage. A plausible interpretation should identify the dominant heat-transfer pathways rather than assuming that every thermally damaged region was directly contacted by flame.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 16

16. Ignition

Ignition is the transition from heating and chemical decomposition to sustained combustion. For many solid fuels, external heat first raises the surface temperature, moisture is removed, pyrolysis begins, combustible gases are generated, and a suitable mixture forms above the surface. A pilot can then initiate gas-phase combustion.

Ignition is therefore a process rather than a single temperature. Heat flux, exposure duration, material thickness, thermal properties, moisture, surface condition, airflow, and the characteristics of the ignition source all matter. A short intense exposure can produce a different response from a long low-intensity exposure.

In forensic analysis, the proposed ignition source must be connected to a mechanism. A lighter found near a damaged object establishes opportunity, not causation. The source must have been capable of delivering energy to the relevant fuel under the actual environmental conditions.

17. Piloted Ignition and Autoignition

Piloted ignition uses an external flame, spark, hot particle, or other localized source to initiate combustion. The pilot supplies energy directly and can create a small reaction zone that grows if surrounding conditions support sustained burning.

Autoignition occurs when heating causes the material or mixture to reach a condition where chemical reaction becomes self-sustaining without an external pilot. The process may involve slow exothermic reactions, thermal decomposition, heat accumulation, and eventually rapid reaction.

The distinction is important because a scene may contain multiple potential sources. If a hypothesis requires autoignition, the investigator should demonstrate a credible heat-accumulation mechanism and sufficient time. If it requires piloted ignition, the energy-transfer pathway from the source to the fuel should be established.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 18

18. Flame Structure and Diffusion Flames

Many everyday fires are diffusion flames. Fuel is released from a surface or opening while oxygen-containing air arrives from the surrounding environment. The fuel and oxidizer mix in and around the reaction zone rather than being completely premixed before ignition.

Flame structure is controlled by transport and chemistry. Temperature, species concentration, soot formation, turbulence, and radiation vary throughout the flame. The luminous yellow appearance of many diffusion flames is associated in part with hot soot particles, while other flames can be much less luminous.

Flame colour should not be treated as a thermometer or a unique fuel identifier. The same fuel can produce different visual characteristics under different mixture and ventilation conditions. Quantitative interpretation requires measurements or validated models rather than visual impression alone.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 19

19. Pyrolysis

Pyrolysis is the thermal decomposition of a material when it is heated without enough oxidizer for complete combustion. It converts a solid or liquid into a mixture of gases, vapours, condensable products, and solid residue. In many solid-fuel fires, pyrolysis supplies the actual gaseous fuel consumed by the flame.

Wood illustrates the process well. Moisture is first heated and evaporated. As temperature rises, the principal organic components undergo decomposition and generate combustible volatiles and char. Heat feedback from the flame increases the rate of this decomposition, producing a self-reinforcing cycle.

Pyrolysis is important to forensic reconstruction because damage to a solid fuel can be caused by heat exposure even before flaming reaches that material. A heated but unignited object can therefore show thermal decomposition or charring as part of preheating and fire spread.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 20

20. Pyrolysis of Polymers and Synthetic Materials

Polymeric materials do not all decompose in the same way. Depending on polymer chemistry, additives, fillers, geometry, and heating rate, a plastic may soften, melt, shrink, drip, depolymerize, fragment, char, or produce a complex mixture of gases and condensable products.

This is why the generic statement 'plastic burns' has little analytical value. A polymer's decomposition products and thermal response depend on its chemical structure. Some materials produce substantial combustible gases; others generate more char or condensed products. Additives can also change behaviour.

Forensic interpretation should identify the actual material where possible and distinguish manufacturer composition from assumptions based solely on appearance. Chemical analysis, product information, physical examination, and the observed thermal environment can be combined to determine whether the material's response is consistent with the proposed fire sequence.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 21

21. Smouldering

Smouldering is a flameless combustion process that propagates through a porous or permeable solid. It generally produces less heat per unit time than flaming combustion but can persist for long periods. Common examples include some upholstered materials, peat, cellulose-rich products, and porous carbonaceous residues.

Smouldering depends strongly on oxygen transport through the material and heat transfer within it. Because the reaction front is relatively slow, local heat accumulation can occur over extended periods. Smouldering can later transition to flaming when ventilation or heat feedback changes.

Forensics requires special care because the absence of a visible flame does not mean combustion has stopped. A smouldering source can survive for hours and ignite nearby material after conditions change. The timing of ventilation, movement of materials, and post-fire disturbance can therefore be important.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 22

22. Fire Growth and Heat Release Rate

Heat release rate (HRR) is the rate at which a fire releases thermal energy, usually expressed in kW or MW. It is a central fire-dynamics variable because it controls, directly or indirectly, plume temperature, convective transport, radiation, smoke production, and the rate at which nearby materials are heated.

The total energy released is the time integral of HRR. A fire with a high peak HRR can create severe thermal exposure even if its duration is short. A lower-HRR fire lasting much longer can deliver substantial total energy while producing a different environment.

Fire growth is therefore not adequately described by flame height alone. A useful analysis asks how HRR changes with time, what fuel is contributing, whether ventilation limits burning, and what heat-transfer pathways are produced by the evolving fire.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 23

23. t-Squared Fire Growth

A commonly used engineering approximation for early fire growth is the t-squared model, expressed as Q̇ = αt², where Q̇ is heat release rate, t is time after ignition, and α is a fire-growth coefficient. The model is useful for categorizing idealized growth as slow, medium, fast, or ultra-fast under defined conventions.

The model is not a universal law of fire development. Real fires are affected by fuel discontinuities, ventilation, suppression, changes in exposed surface, flame spread, and compartment geometry. Once these factors become dominant, the simple t-squared curve can become a poor representation.

Its value is therefore conceptual and engineering-oriented: it provides a reproducible way to discuss the rate at which a fire might grow during an early phase. It should not be used to reverse-engineer an exact origin time from a photograph without supporting evidence.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 24

24. Fuel-Controlled and Ventilation-Controlled Fires

A fuel-controlled fire has enough oxidizer available that the burning rate is governed mainly by how quickly fuel can be supplied to the reaction zone. An open fire with abundant air may approximate this condition.

A ventilation-controlled fire is limited by the supply of oxidizer. This can occur in an enclosed compartment where doors and windows restrict airflow. Burning may become incomplete, smoke production can increase, and carbon monoxide concentrations can rise. Introducing a new opening can then change the fire dramatically.

The distinction is not permanent. A fire can move from fuel-controlled to ventilation-controlled and back again as windows fail, doors open, fuel is consumed, or suppression changes the environment. Fire reconstruction must therefore treat ventilation as a time-dependent variable.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 25

25. Fire Plumes and Buoyancy

A fire plume is a rising flow generated by buoyancy above a heat source. Hot combustion products are less dense than surrounding air and therefore rise, entraining additional air as they move. The plume transports heat and combustion products into the upper region of a compartment or the atmosphere.

The plume is not simply a column of smoke. It contains a mixture whose temperature, density, velocity, and composition change with height. Entrainment increases the mass flow while generally reducing the temperature relative to the source region.

Plume behaviour helps explain why ceilings and upper walls can receive substantial thermal exposure even when the flame is located lower in the room. It also explains the movement of smoke toward openings and the formation of hot upper layers.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 26

26. Ventilation and Airflow

Ventilation controls the movement of air into and combustion products out of a fire environment. Openings can include doors, windows, shafts, cracks, ducts, roof vents, and mechanically driven systems. Their size, position, height, and timing influence the pressure and flow field.

A ventilation change can have several simultaneous effects. It may increase oxygen delivery, change flame location, alter plume direction, reduce or increase smoke accumulation, and change heat exposure. Therefore, opening a door is not merely an 'air supply' event; it changes the boundary conditions of the entire fire system.

Forensic documentation should record the condition of openings and, where possible, their sequence of change. Statements such as 'the room was well ventilated' are too vague for rigorous reconstruction.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 27

27. Smoke and Combustion Products

Smoke is a mixture of gases, vapours, and suspended particles produced by combustion and pyrolysis. Its composition varies with fuel chemistry and burning conditions. Soot is one important particulate component, but smoke also contains condensable organic compounds, water, carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, and numerous fuel-specific species.

Smoke movement is governed by buoyancy, entrainment, pressure differences, ventilation, and temperature gradients. Deposition on surfaces depends on particle concentration, surface temperature, airflow, and condensation processes. Consequently, a soot deposit is a record of transport and deposition as well as combustion.

Chemical findings can support a fire hypothesis, but they should be interpreted within the physical scene. Detecting a combustion product generally indicates that combustion or thermal decomposition occurred; it does not automatically establish where ignition occurred or which fuel was first involved.

SUBJECT 02 · CORE STUDY NOTE 28

28. Carbon Monoxide and Incomplete Combustion

Carbon monoxide is a major product of incomplete combustion of carbon-containing fuels. Its formation becomes important when oxidation is incomplete, particularly in oxygen-deficient or poorly mixed regions. Carbon monoxide is hazardous because it interferes with oxygen transport in the body.

Its concentration in a fire environment is not determined by one variable. Fuel chemistry, temperature, equivalence ratio, mixing, ventilation, and residence time all contribute. A well-ventilated flame can still produce some CO, while an oxygen-starved region can produce substantially more.

From a forensic perspective, CO measurements can contribute to reconstruction of fire conditions, especially when combined with ventilation and combustion evidence. However, post-fire transport and later ventilation can alter concentrations, so a measured value must be interpreted with its sampling history and scene conditions.

29. Fire Decay and Post-Flashover Conditions

As fuel is consumed, oxygen becomes depleted, heat losses increase, or suppression removes energy, the fire may enter a decay phase. Flaming can decrease while hot surfaces, embers, and smouldering material remain. A fire can therefore continue to pose an ignition hazard even when the visible flame is small.

In compartment fires, the thermal environment can become extremely severe when much of the available fuel participates in combustion. Flashover is a rapid transition to widespread involvement of exposed combustible surfaces driven by intense heat feedback. It is not simply a synonym for 'a large fire' and should be treated as a specific fire-dynamics phenomenon.

Post-flashover damage is particularly difficult to interpret as an origin indicator because intense thermal exposure can affect large portions of a compartment. Origin analysis must therefore consider the complete fire-development sequence rather than ranking the most damaged location as the origin.

30. Fire as a Time-Dependent System

A fire scene is the final state of a process that changed continuously with time. Fuel was heated, vapours were generated, flames developed, gases moved, ventilation changed, materials were consumed, and suppression or structural failure may have interrupted the sequence.

This temporal view is central to forensic reasoning. A pattern should be interpreted as an effect produced during a particular stage of the fire, not as an independent signature. For example, a surface can be heated by an upper hot layer after the flame front has moved elsewhere. Likewise, soot can be deposited after the first fuel has already been consumed.

A credible reconstruction therefore asks: what happened first, what changed next, what physical mechanism produced each observation, and what evidence supports the proposed sequence? The objective is not to find a single 'magic pattern' but to build and test a coherent physical explanation.
